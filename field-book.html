CHAPTER 1 — BEFORE THERE WAS A GARDEN

January–February 2026

In January, YAGI was still a question being worked out among people, places, and possibilities. Could a school rooted in yoga and community learning create a garden that served as an open-air classroom? What would it take to make that classroom accessible, practical, and sustained by more than one person's effort?

An early planning overview prepared by Teagan Rowe explored crop choices, sunlight, spacing, possible sites, and seed-starting dates. The January 29 meeting record discussed raised beds measuring eight by four feet, untreated pine, permission before digging, and two possible locations: a preschool site near Fond du Lac and 25th, and an existing garden at Casa Maria. The team also considered outreach to Riverwest Co-op, volunteer registration, and modest fundraising. Those were possibilities under discussion, not a finished garden.

The early documents remind us that beginning with a plan does not mean knowing where we will end up. A good first plan gives people something to examine together. It should be specific enough to test, but open enough to change when a site, season, or community offers new information.

TRY THIS WHERE YOU LIVE

Write one page describing whom your garden hopes to serve and why. Visit possible locations before purchasing materials. Ask who owns the land, who can authorize cultivation, how water will be accessed, and who will return when the first enthusiasm fades. Keep your first plan dated. Later, it will tell you how much you learned.

CHAPTER 2 — STARTING SMALL

Riverwest and Early Spring 2026

Before Garden Park became the center of the work, YAGI's learning was already happening in Riverwest. Teagan helped begin seedlings in her backyard, and the school held outdoor activations while exploring where the garden could take root. A garden can begin in trays and conversations long before anyone has permission to turn a permanent bed.

The early planting calendar names February 24 and March 3 as indoor seed-starting dates. These dates are documented in the plan; we still need to compare them with attendance, Jibble notes, and photographs before treating them as verified completed sessions. That distinction matters. A calendar shows intention; a field record shows what happened.

Starting in a backyard allowed the group to begin learning about timing, care, and growth without waiting for every larger question to be settled. It also introduced a recurring YAGI lesson: stewardship grows through relationships. People lend space, share tools, explain a technique, or simply show up again.

TRY THIS WHERE YOU LIVE

Start a small number of labeled trays. Record the seed, sowing date, light, watering, and germination. Take a photograph from the same angle each week. If you plan to transplant into a community site, check that the site and care schedule will be ready before the plants outgrow their containers.

CHAPTER 3 — FINDING GROUND

Permission, Place, and Planning

Finding land is not the same as receiving permission to work it. Early YAGI meetings considered several locations and the practical need for city approval. The May 27 meeting notes discuss permit paperwork, a provisional restoration allocation of approximately $500–$650, volunteer recruitment, and the need to acquire a lawnmower. By June 10, the restoration report describes the first workday as following city approval.

The chronology is useful precisely because it was not effortless. Before beds could be restored, the school had to ask what work was allowed, how volunteers would be welcomed, what tools were needed, and how a limited budget could support the first phase. The team planned to start with a manageable portion of the site rather than restore everything at once.

Permission should be documented, not assumed. The precise permit name, application steps, dates, and any conditions will be added after reviewing the city's correspondence and the school's records.

TRY THIS WHERE YOU LIVE

Identify the landowner and local agency responsible for the site. Ask about cultivation, digging, water, structures, soil safety, public access, and maintenance. Keep copies of permissions and record any restrictions. Make a first-phase budget that includes cleanup, protective equipment, tools, soil, and an allowance for the unexpected.

CHAPTER 4 — THE FIRST RESTORATION

June 10, 2026

The first recorded Phase 1 restoration session did not unfold exactly as originally imagined. Days before the event, Luan and Anyssa experienced a personal safety incident that limited their ability to take part on-site. The team did not cancel. Sammy and Teagan adapted the plan, led a smaller workday, and concentrated on what could actually be completed.

Three Food Not Bombs volunteers joined them. Together, the five participants worked from 10 a.m. to 1 p.m., contributing approximately fifteen collective service hours. They used hoes and shovels, removed unwanted growth, cleared beds, cleaned the site, and prepared soil for future planting. Four beds were fully cleared for the next stage; two more were partly cleared. Food Not Bombs volunteers also contributed water, oranges, snacks, and hospitality.

This was more than a successful cleanup. It showed how leadership could continue when the founder was absent, and how mutual aid could become part of ecological education. The work did not require a perfect event. It required a clear, achievable task and people willing to carry it together.

TRY THIS WHERE YOU LIVE

For your first restoration day, choose only a few tasks that can be finished safely. Begin with a site walk and a shared safety briefing. Identify plants before pulling them where possible. Keep tools visible and supervised. Record who participated, the collective work accomplished, and what must wait for the next visit. Never confuse group volunteer hours with an individual's hours.

CHAPTER 5 — LEARNING WHAT WAS ALREADY GROWING

Plants, Soil, and Debris

A neglected bed can look like a single mass of green until we begin to notice its differences. Some plants may be crops from an earlier season. Others may be spontaneous seedlings, valuable habitat plants, aggressive competitors, or introduced species that need careful management. The act of clearing a bed should begin with looking.

YAGI's June 24 coordination notes discuss invasive-species observations, clover, soil improvement, and ways to reduce mowing. A later plant record notes red clover near pollinator habitat. Red clover can provide forage for pollinators, but it is introduced in Wisconsin; usefulness and native status are not the same thing. Likewise, a weed is not necessarily invasive. We must identify a plant and understand its behavior before giving it that label.

The site's history also appeared in debris, worn infrastructure, and the condition of the soil. A June 26 assessment checklist called for removing major debris and identifying weeds or invasive species. The July 15 journal records trash cleanup and sorting scrap wood near the swing set. The work of restoration included asking what could be safely reused, what needed removal, and what should be left undisturbed until identified.

TRY THIS WHERE YOU LIVE

Photograph a plant in its setting, then capture its leaves, stem, flowers or fruit, and growth pattern. Record the location and date. Compare the observation with a reliable regional flora or extension reference. Mark identification as confirmed, probable, or unknown. Do not eat, transplant, compost, or apply a treatment to an unidentified plant simply because it resembles a familiar species. For suspected invasive plants, check local guidance before disturbing them; some species spread through fragments or seed.

CHAPTER 6 — KEEPING A GARDEN ALIVE

Summer 2026

Clearing beds is a beginning, not a care system. As the garden moved toward planting and cultivation, its needs multiplied: watering, weed control, pest observation, mowing, protection, and records that could survive a missed meeting. The team developed responsibilities while doing the work, not in a single administrative meeting.

By August 19, the leadership notes named Luan, Teagan, Sammy, and Anyssa as current garden leads and emphasized a repeatable rhythm: observe, document, assign, care, follow up, and return. The September 2 meeting then tested more specialized pathways through Garden Operations, Foodways, and Arts & Storytelling. These roles grew from lived responsibilities. They were not prerequisites to starting the garden.

One preserved garden record describes volunteer squash lost during mowing. That mistake became a simple, durable lesson: identify and mark valuable spontaneous growth before maintenance crews or volunteers mow. A garden needs shared understanding as much as it needs tools.

TRY THIS WHERE YOU LIVE

At the end of every workday, write down three things: what is alive and needs attention, who will return, and what they will check. Mark no-mow areas and newly emerging plants clearly. Assign a backup for essential care. Keep the record short enough that someone will actually use it.

CHAPTER 7 — A GARDEN WITHIN A NEIGHBORHOOD

Access, Loss, and Care

Garden Park was never a sealed classroom. It was a place within a living neighborhood, carrying the realities of public access, discarded materials, shared use, and uneven resources. The July 15 field journal describes trash cleanup across the property, scrap wood gathered for possible future use, and the discovery that a trash receptacle near the entrance had been stolen. The area around the water totes, by contrast, was reported clean and well cared for.

The school also experienced the loss of two lawnmowers—one purchased and one donated—within roughly two weeks, according to the founder's account. The team subsequently stopped leaving equipment on-site. Exact purchase, donation, and loss dates still need to be matched to inventory and meeting records. The May 27 meeting already shows how significant acquiring a mower had been to the restoration plan.

The site also prompted observations concerning homelessness and people occupying or using public space. Those observations deserve careful, humane documentation. The presence of people without housing is not evidence of theft or littering. A responsible field record distinguishes what was directly seen, what was reported, what posed a specific hazard, and what remains unknown. Community stewardship requires both practical boundaries and respect for the people who share a neighborhood.

TRY THIS WHERE YOU LIVE

Plan secure off-site storage before buying equipment. Log purchases, donations, checkouts, and losses. Make cleanup routines safe: do not handle needles, unknown chemicals, or suspicious materials without appropriate training and municipal support. If people are present on the land, approach with dignity, follow site agreements, and document concrete access or safety issues without making assumptions about identity or responsibility.

CHAPTER 8 — HARVEST, FOODWAYS, AND THE WIDER CLASSROOM

Summer–Autumn 2026

YAGI's classroom extended beyond its beds. While developing the school garden, Luan volunteered in kind at Darius Simmons Garden, learned through service at the Gathering Table at All Peoples Church, and received informal gardening advice from Milwaukee gardener Susan Holty. These relationships mattered because ecological learning was not confined to the work of planting. It also involved preparing food, sharing resources, listening to experienced neighbors, and seeing how other community spaces sustained themselves.

The first season included harvest and food-sharing observations, compost learning, and exchanges between sites. Public field lessons later recorded kale harvesting, composting, and the return of nourishment to soil. The school's Patreon lessons are companion windows into this practice, not substitutes for the written account.

In October, a Spanish-speaking family of seven visited the garden, and four young people participated in compost learning. The moment illustrates the possibility of a garden as a multilingual community classroom, where people can join through action before they know the school's vocabulary.

TRY THIS WHERE YOU LIVE

Volunteer with an existing garden or food program before launching a new one. Ask what they have learned about harvest, waste, and consistent care. Record who taught you a technique and credit them accurately. If you share food, establish safe handling and clear distribution practices. Invite participants to contribute knowledge in the languages they use.

CHAPTER 9 — WHEN THE GARDEN ASKED US TO SLOW DOWN

September–October 2026

By early September, the garden was asking us to look more closely. Of the four beds examined on September 5, two needed a fresh start. Bed 1 had struggled to establish, and volunteer plants had taken over much of the space. No intentional crops could be identified in Bed 4 during that review. Yet Bed 2 was still growing: its brassicas were vigorous, even as insects left holes in their leaves. In Bed 3, scattered seedlings and a dense cluster of new growth deserved patience rather than a quick clearing.

We had planted with hope, but the plants kept growing on days when no one was there. Insects fed, weeds appeared, weather tested the temporary covers, and young seedlings needed attention between gatherings. When a weekly field day was missed, the garden did not wait for us. The review made something plain: a planting schedule was not yet the same thing as a dependable rhythm of care.

Long strips of duct tape holding temporary plastic in place had not stood up reliably to weather. The team began thinking instead about reusable hoops, clips, and netting. Bed 2 was marked for protection; Bed 3 for careful recovery; Beds 1 and 4 for reset. A possible $200 recovery budget was discussed, but purchasing was held until the group could establish who would return to care for what was planted.

The lesson was not that we should never make mistakes. It was that we should learn to distinguish a living plant that could recover from an empty space we were not yet prepared to maintain. More beds would not solve a shortage of dependable observation.

TRY THIS WHERE YOU LIVE

Walk every active bed with a notebook. Mark it preserve, recover, reset, or observe further. Record pest evidence without guessing the cause. Begin with low-risk measures such as hand removal of visible pests and appropriate physical barriers. Do not apply an untested vinegar spray to valuable crop foliage. Before planting again, name the primary steward, the backup, and the next inspection date.

CHAPTER 10 — PASSING THE SEASON FORWARD

Winter 2026 and Year Two

The first season did not end with a perfect garden. It ended with a clearer understanding of what a garden asks of its people. Soil, seeds, and donated materials helped begin the work. Relationships kept it possible. The failures made the next questions sharper: How many beds can we responsibly tend? What must be repaired before spring? Who knows where the tools and records are? How can the next volunteer learn without depending on the founder's memory?

The winter planning record includes proposed bed repairs and upgrades, better protective covers, signage, and clearer stewardship coverage. Larger ideas—such as a mural, greenhouse, and solar-powered community stage—belong to future phases, not to the list of 2026 accomplishments. The school can dream expansively while documenting the difference between an idea, an approved plan, and a completed task.

The most valuable inheritance from Season One is not a fixed organizational chart. It is a habit of paying attention, returning, and making knowledge shareable. If another neighborhood can use these pages to begin a small garden and avoid even a few of our mistakes, then the first season continues bearing fruit.

TRY THIS WHERE YOU LIVE

At the end of your first season, gather the people who cared for the site. Ask what survived, what failed, what was lost, and what they would do differently. Choose only the repairs and plantings your team can maintain. Make a simple handoff: site map, contacts, tool location, bed notes, and next workday.

CHAPTER 11 — WHAT THE FIRST SEASON TAUGHT US

A Field-to-Stewardship Transition | November 2026–2027

The garden was never only a collection of raised beds. It was a meeting place between soil, neighborhood history, practical labor, donated resources, public administration, and the people who returned to care for it. By the end of its inaugural growing season, YAGI had learned that planting is only one part of keeping a garden alive.

The first lesson was to begin with what exists. In spring, the school made seed-starting containers from materials at hand, first using cut plastic cups and later turning to scrap cardboard tied with string. In the garden itself, existing bed frames, found materials, and painted artwork were adapted to support restoration. This ingenuity reduced the need for purchases, but also introduced a responsibility: salvaged materials must be assessed for sharp edges, contamination, durability, and suitability near soil and food.

The second lesson was that soil holds a site's history. Heavy clay, persistent bindweed, and worn frames made restoration slower than expected. During an August tree transplant near the rear of the garden, the founder encountered gravel mixed with buried plastic. No laboratory assessment or full excavation survey was completed, so the observation should not be mistaken for a diagnosis of contamination. It does justify careful inspection before future planting, particularly where edible crops or deeper excavation are proposed.

The third lesson was that a water source cannot remain an assumption. Garden-permit correspondence began in May, while hydrant access required a separate referral and application process in June. The team carried water and sought alternative arrangements while awaiting a usable resolution. For 2027, the immediate priority is a dependable, permitted water supply with a backup plan. A freestanding roof-fed rainwater harvesting station is a proposed Phase 2 educational and irrigation project, not a completed or approved substitute.

The fourth lesson was to protect the garden's borders. Repeated litter management, deteriorating fencing, and the accidental mowing of plants outside the fence showed that the edge of a garden is a place of shared responsibility. Phase 1 therefore proposes fence repairs, improved signs, and additional border trash receptacles supported by a real collection schedule. Existing donated wooden aluminum-can recycling stations should be inventoried separately from new trash-bin purchases.

The fifth lesson was that compost needs both creativity and continuity. The first season used improvised structures, including a reused box frame contributed through Darius Simmons Garden. These adaptations helped the school learn what worked, but they are not equivalent to a completed permanent compost system. Phase 1 proposes a durable, maintainable station, potentially built from safe reclaimed pallets, with designated responsibilities for material intake, turning, curing, contamination prevention, and upkeep.

The sixth lesson was that nourishment could grow from donated resources even before the garden produced enough food to supply meals. Sammy began preparing YAGI dishes with produce contributed through the Gathering Table. Later, during field days, the school set up an impromptu bread-sharing shelf using bread donated by Rocket Baby Bakery and the Gathering Table. The temporary shelf was not a permanent food pantry, and donated food should not be represented as Garden Park harvest. The deeper accomplishment was connecting ecological learning to hospitality and direct service.

The seventh lesson was reciprocity between organizations. Requests for water guidance, tools, and volunteer support led to conversations with Groundwork Milwaukee about offering YAGI volunteers, yoga, educational activities, and light hospitality to other gardens. The Ecological Wellness Support Tour developed as a serious proposal, but its complete delivery is not verified. The school learned to design small offerings that respect partner capacity rather than expecting already-busy organizations to coordinate everything.

The eighth lesson was to practice compassion without making assumptions. Encounters with housing insecurity raised questions about belongings, temporary rest, cleanliness, and shared public space. Ideas for a temporary belongings station and cardboard insulation were discussed, but their actual implementation remains unverified. A garden's response should protect dignity and safety, respect consent and site permissions, and connect neighbors with appropriate outreach services. Litter management and homelessness are not interchangeable problems.

THE 2027 STEWARDSHIP METHOD

At the start of each field day, the steward reviews water availability, weather, tool condition, access paths, compost condition, bed priorities, and any safety concerns. Volunteers are welcomed into tasks they can complete safely, with clear explanations of why the work matters. A brief photograph and dated observation should be recorded before major changes.

During the session, the team distinguishes restoration from routine care. Restoration includes repairing frames, removing unsafe debris, and preparing infrastructure. Routine care includes watering, weeding, monitoring plant health, compost maintenance, and respectful waste collection. New construction, fence alterations, water connections, or changes affecting shared space require the relevant permissions before work begins.

At the end of the day, the steward records what was done, who participated with consent, which materials were purchased or contributed, and what remains unfinished. Jibble labor records and the field observation portal serve different purposes and should not be conflated. A task is not complete merely because it appears in a plan.

THE FOUR QUESTIONS FOR EVERY MATERIAL

1. Where did it come from? Record purchased, donated, salvaged from discarded materials, or already present on-site.

2. Is it safe and suitable? Inspect for contamination, treatment, damage, sharp edges, and appropriate use.

3. What did it become? Record the actual installed use, temporary experiment, storage location, or proposed future use.

4. Who will maintain it? Assign a steward and backup before relying on it as permanent infrastructure.

These questions protect the school's financial integrity as well as its ecological philosophy. The historical Phase 1 tracker was corrected from $532 to $492 in recorded actual costs after the founder confirmed that a $40 contingency was never spent. The tracker is a historical accounting record, not a full valuation of volunteer labor or donated materials. Seeds received from Barb at Near West Side Partners and through AgCon sources for 2027 belong in a separate in-kind seed inventory, with varieties, quantities, dates, and donor identities verified where possible.

AN APPRENTICE FIELD EXERCISE — FROM OBSERVATION TO PROPOSAL

Choose one visible challenge: water access, a damaged fence segment, a litter-prone border, a neglected bed, or compost management. Make a dated observation without assigning blame. Photograph the condition with permission. Identify what is already available, what would need to be donated or purchased, and whose approval is necessary. Draft the smallest safe intervention and a maintenance plan. Return after the next stewardship session to assess whether the intervention worked. If it did not, record that too.

The purpose is not to produce a perfect garden in a single season. It is to develop people who can notice carefully, act responsibly, share resources fairly, and leave clear records for those who come next.
